# The Ribbon Harbor Notebook

By Nera Sample. Entirely invented for a tooling evaluation.

[p.1]

## Chapter 1: A gate made of ribbons

Ribbon Harbor is an invented tabletop rehearsal, not a description of a real port. Two keepers move paper ribbons between three cardboard berths. A blue token means that a gate is being examined; an amber vane remembers the direction chosen in the last trial. The game has no weather simulation. These objects are deliberately unlike the signals of working harbors. This notebook describes a tiny rehearsal procedure, not maritime advice. A keeper who needs to decide whether a real vessel may sail must leave this notebook and consult the responsible authority.

[p.2]

## The gate token

Place the blue token beside the gate before moving a ribbon. Name the gate aloud and ask the other keeper to point to that same gate. If the two fingers indicate different gates, stop without moving the ribbon. Write both names on the scratch sheet. Rename the gates until both keepers point to one location. The product of this procedure is an agreed gate identity, not permission to open it. A correct name does not establish capacity, fairness, or safety.

[p.3]

## When the token travels

Only the keeper holding the scratch sheet moves the blue token. The second keeper observes and points. After each move, both keepers say which gate is now under examination. If the token is hidden by a ribbon, uncover it before continuing. No extra turn is awarded for noticing the hidden token. The scratch sheet may be a plain rectangle of paper. Its shape is not part of the method; the identity of its current holder is.

[p.4]

## Two similar names

In a fabricated rehearsal, the labels North Fold and Near Fold sounded alike. One keeper pointed left while the other touched the right-hand berth. They did not count this as a failed player. They wrote the two names side by side, changed Near Fold to Small Fold, and repeated the pointing step. Only the second pointing produced agreement. This example illustrates the gate-token procedure; it does not show that any pair of labels will be understood by every player.

[p.5]

## What the gate record contains

The gate record contains the old label, the replacement label if any, and the place both keepers pointed to. It also records whether a ribbon concealed the token. Recording a time is optional. The notebook supplies no speed target or acceptable disagreement rate. A designer may add such a target for a separate study, but must not attribute it to the gate-token procedure. A blank record is different from an agreement record.

[p.6]

## A contrary rehearsal

Another invented pair moved the ribbon first and argued about names afterward. Their final drawing looked tidy, but neither could tell which gate had been selected before the move. They repeated the rehearsal from the last agreed drawing. The order mattered: naming and pointing preceded movement. A photograph taken at the end cannot recover a missing earlier agreement. This is a counterexample to skipping the pointing step, not evidence that photographs are generally useless.

[p.7]

## Leaving chapter one

Use the gate-token procedure when players refer to similar-looking places and the next action depends on selecting one place. Do not use it to choose between strategic plans or to decide who should win. An agreed identity is the input to the vane trial in chapter two. If the places cannot be distinguished even after renaming, report that condition rather than inventing an agreement. The remaining chapters never waive this condition.

[p.8]

## Chapter 2: One berth at a time

A vane trial compares two arrangements at one identified berth. A trial begins with the gate record from chapter one, a visible amber vane, and an empty ledger row. The keepers choose the berth before changing anything. Ribbons at other berths remain as they were. The rehearsal is useful for isolating a confusing local signal. It cannot certify an entire harbor, since it deliberately leaves neighboring berths untested.

[p.9]

## Changing the vane

Turn one amber vane and copy its previous direction into the ledger. Then draw the new direction in the next column. Keep the ribbon positions fixed during this comparison. Ask the observing keeper which gate the vane points toward. Record the answer without correcting it in advance. The output is a before-and-after direction record and an observed answer. The notebook supplies no numeric score for the answer.

[p.10]

## The limit of a local trial

Apply the vane trial to one berth only; it never authorizes opening the whole harbor. If the observing keeper identifies a different gate, return the vane to its recorded previous direction and inspect the gate names. If the keeper identifies the intended gate, retain the drawing as a local observation. Even several matching answers remain observations of that berth. A global opening decision needs criteria that this notebook does not provide.

[p.11]

## A folded arrow

In a fabricated example, a folded amber arrow appeared to point toward two berths. The keepers copied its previous direction before unfolding it. After a single turn, the observer still named the other gate. They restored the old direction and returned to the labels. The record preserved a failed local observation. They did not change a second vane to rescue the result, because that would erase which change was being examined.

[p.12]

## The missing decoration

An optional decoration was a tiny crescent printed on the vane. Its original illustration is absent from this invented text. The procedure above does not depend on that decoration: direction is drawn in the ledger. A designer who wants to reproduce the crescent must mark the missing illustration rather than claim that the author supplied its dimensions. Missing visual detail and an unspecified success threshold are different limitations.

[p.13]

## A useful difference

A local trial is different from a vote. The observing keeper describes a direction, not a preference for one keeper's plan. The ledger has columns for the previous direction, the changed direction, the named gate, and whether restoration was required. Those columns preserve the procedure's inputs and outputs. Additional project notes can be appended, but must be labeled as additions rather than implied conditions of the book's method.

[p.14]

## Leaving chapter two

Before summarizing a vane trial, reread chapter one and the limit on page ten. A shared gate name remains necessary. An answer about the whole harbor is outside the local trial's scope. The next chapter explains how to close a rehearsal without hiding an unresolved observation. It does not turn an unresolved local answer into approval. The keepers may stop with an incomplete record; incompleteness must remain visible.

[p.15]

## Chapter 3: A visible closing record

A closing record separates an observed arrangement from a plan to try later. Its inputs are the last agreed gate drawing, the local vane ledger, and the ribbon still on the table. One keeper reads the gate name and the other points to the final ribbon. If these disagree, the record stays open. Neither the color of the paper nor the neatness of the drawing decides whether the record is complete.

[p.16]

## Closing without erasure

Keep the final ribbon visible until both harbor keepers sign the ledger. Read the last named gate, point to the ribbon, and compare it with the drawing. Copy any unresolved answer into a separate open-question row before signing. The signatures acknowledge what was recorded, not the quality of the design. If either keeper cannot identify the ribbon, do not erase or sign; return to the last agreed drawing.

[p.17]

## A disagreement at closing

In a fabricated rehearsal, the ledger named Small Fold while the final ribbon lay beside Wide Fold. The keepers left both objects visible, added an open-question row, and declined to sign. They did not average the two names or let the more confident keeper choose. A later rehearsal began from their last agreed drawing. This example shows preservation of uncertainty, not a claim about the best organizational decision process.

[p.18]

## The empty berth as a story

An empty cardboard berth can suggest a promise, a departure, or room for an unexpected visitor. These are interpretive observations. The notebook offers no procedure for selecting the correct interpretation and no test for how strongly players should feel it. A designer may discuss the image as an authorial viewpoint. It should remain available as reference material, rather than be converted into a mandatory three-step method for producing an emotion.

[p.19]

## A name for an open question

An open-question row is a place to preserve an unresolved observation beside the agreed record. It is not a task automatically assigned to the next player and not a penalty. The phrase quiet berth names the empty-berth viewpoint on page eighteen; it has no scoring role. Keeping these meanings apart prevents a reference metaphor from turning into an execution rule. The notebook deliberately offers no recommended number of quiet berths.

[p.20]

## The last page

The three procedures form a small chain: establish one gate identity, inspect one local vane change, and preserve a visible closing record. The empty-berth viewpoint remains a separate reference. Nothing in the chain estimates market success, measures player enjoyment, or authorizes a real harbor operation. When a needed criterion is absent, state that absence and provide any independent design suggestion under its own name. Repeating the chain does not make those missing criteria appear.
